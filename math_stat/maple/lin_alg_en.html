Linear Algebra

In this section, we briefly introduce the LinearAlgebra library to perform major tasks in Linear Algebra.

Basic

The Maple functions for linear algebra are in the following library. 

> with(LinearAlgebra):

It is easy to create (column) vectors and matrices.

> v := Vector([3, 2, -1]);
\[ v := \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> A := Matrix([[1 , 2 ,3],[2 ,3 ,4],[3,4,5]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \]

We can add and multiply vectors and matrices as long as you respects the rules about dimensions.

>  u := A v;
\[ u := \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 8 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> w := u + 2*v;
\[ w := \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 12 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix} \]

It is easy to retrieve and modify entries of a vector or a matrix.

> w[2]; A[2.3];
\[ \begin{split} & 12 \\ & 4 \end{split} \] 
> A[2,3] := 7; A;
\[ \begin{split} & A_{2,3} := 7 \\ & \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 7 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

Euclidean Geometry

The scalar product of the vectors u and v is given by

>  DotProduct(u,v);
\[ 16 \]

The Euclidean norm, the 1-norm and the infinite norm of the vector v are:

> norm(v, 2); norm(v, 1); norm(v, infinity);
\[ \begin{split} \sqrt{14} \\ 6 \\ 3 \end{split} \]

The Cross product of u and v is given by

> w := CrossProduct(u,v);
\[ w := \begin{bmatrix} -31 \\ 40 \\-16 \end{bmatrix} \]

It is well known that w is orthogonal to u and v. 

> DotProduct(w,u); DotProduct(w,v);
\[ \begin{split} & 0 \\ & 0 \end{split} \]

Matrix Operations

We have given above the basic method to define a matrix in Maple. There are many more methods to define matrices. Special matrices may have their own function to create them. Here are some examples.

> Id: = IdentityMatrix(3);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> Matrix(3, 4, {(1, 2) = 3, (1, 3) = 5, (3, 4) = 7}, fill = 1);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1& 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

There is also a shorthand notation to define a matrix column by column. 

> B := <<1, 4> |<3, 7>>;
\[ B := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

or row by row

> D := <<1| 4 > ,<3| 7>>;
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

There are more sophisticated methods to create matrices.

> DiagonalMatrix([A, B]);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 7 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

Here are some of the operations on matrices:

> C := InverseMatrix(B);
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{7}{5} & \frac{3}{5} \\ \frac{4}{5} & -\frac{1}{5} \end{bmatrix} \] 
> Transpose(B);
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> P, L, U := LUDecomposition(A); P.L.U ;
\[ \begin{split} & P, L, U := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \\ & \qquad \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 7 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

There are a lot more functions in the LinearAlgebra library: Adjoint, Basis, CharacteristicPolynomial, ColumnDimension, ColumnSpace, Determinant, Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, GaussianElimination, GramSchmidt, JordanForm, MatrixExponential, NullSpace, ReducedRowEchelonForm, and many more.

Note: The function Eigenvectors with the option output = 'list' returns a list where each element of the list is associated to a distinct eigenvalue. Each element of the list is formed of an eigenvalue, its algebraic multiplicity and an independent set of eigenvectors associated to the eigenvalue.

It is possible to apply any function to each component of a matrix or vector. Consider the following function.

> mf:= x-> x^3-x;
\[ mf := x \mapsto x^3 - x \]

To apply mf to each components of B, we use the Maple function map( ).

> map(mf,B);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 24 \\ 60 & 336 \end{bmatrix} \]

Systems of Linear Equations

Consider the system of linear equations.

> eq1 := 3*x+2*y-z=1; eq2 := -x+2*y+z=0; eq3 := -x-y-z=3;
\[ \begin{split} eq1 &:= 3 x+2 y-z = 1 \\ eq2 &:= -x+2 y+z = 0 \\ eq3 &:= -x-y-z = 3 \end{split} \]

To determine if the system has a unique solution, we can compute the determinant of the matrix formed of the coefficients.

> A : <<3,-1,-1>|<2,2,-1>|<-1,1,-1>>:
> Determinat(A)
\[ -10 \]

Since the determinant is different of zero, there is a unique solution. To find the solution, we use

> sol:=solve({eq1,eq2,eq3},{x,y,z});
\[ sol := \left\{z = -\frac{27}{10}, y = \frac{4}{5}, x = -\frac{11}{10} \right\} \]

To retrieve the values of x, y and z for this set, we use the Maple command subs().

> x := subs(sol,x); y := subs(sol,y); z := subs(sol,z);
\[ \begin{split} x &:= -\frac{11}{10} \\ y &:= \frac{4}{5} \\ z &:= -\frac{27}{10} \end{split} \]
