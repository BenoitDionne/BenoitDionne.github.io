Linear Algebra

Other Topics

In this document, we briefly introduce the LinearAlgebra library that helps us perform standard operations in Linear Algebra. There is a sub-package to the LinearAlgebra library that can be used for teaching linear algebra. It is called Student:-LinearAlgebra. This package has some limitations. In particular, numerical computations are not performed with the maximal accuracy normally used by Maple libraries. We will not talk about this sub-package in the present document.

Basic

The Maple functions for linear algebra are in the following library.

> with(LinearAlgebra):

Replace the colon at the end of the previous command by a semi-colon to get a complete list of the functions in that library.

It is easy to create (column) vectors and matrices.

> v := Vector([3, 2, -1]);
\[ v := \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> A := Matrix([[1 , 2 ,3],[2 ,3 ,4],[3,4,5]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \]

We can add and multiply vectors and matrices as long as we respects the rules about dimensions.

>  u := A.v;
\[ u := \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 8 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix} \]

Note: As expected, there is always an exception to the rules. We can add a scalar \(s\) to a matrix \(A\). The result is a matrix that has the same dimensions than the matrix \(A\) and where every component of the new matrix is the result of adding the scalar \(s\) to the every component of the matrix \(A\).

The use of the dot (.) to denote the multiplication of matrices and vectors is optional but strongly recommended. The dot must not be confused with the star (*) which denotes the component by component product of matrices and vectors of the same dimensions. 

> w := u + 2*v;
\[ w := \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 12 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix} \]

It is easy to retrieve and modify components of vectors or matrices.

> w[2]; A[2.3];
\[ \begin{split} & 12 \\ & 4 \end{split} \] 
> A[2,3] := 7; A;
\[ \begin{split} & A_{2,3} := 7 \\ & \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 7 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

Euclidean Geometry

The scalar product of the vectors u and v is computed with the following command.

>  DotProduct(u,v);
\[ 16 \]

Note: There is again an exception to the rules. The dot (.) can be used to denote the scalar product of two vectors of the same dimensions. For this reason, if we really want a row (column) vector to be treated as a matrix with one row (column), then we must define it as matrix. If u and v had been matrices with one row, then u.v would not have been possible.

The Euclidean norm, the 1-norm and the infinite norm of the vector v are:

> norm(v, 2); norm(v, 1); norm(v, infinity);
\[ \begin{split} \sqrt{14} \\ 6 \\ 3 \end{split} \]

The Cross product of u and v is computed with the following command.

> w := CrossProduct(u,v);
\[ w := \begin{bmatrix} -31 \\ 40 \\-16 \end{bmatrix} \]

It is well known that w is orthogonal to u and v. 

> DotProduct(w,u); DotProduct(w,v);
\[ \begin{split} & 0 \\ & 0 \end{split} \]

Matrix Operations

We have given above the basic method to define matrices in Maple. There are many more methods to define matrices. Special matrices may have their own functions in Maple to create them. Here are some examples.

> Id: = IdentityMatrix(3);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> Matrix(3, 4, {(1, 2) = 3, (1, 3) = 5, (3, 4) = 7}, fill = 1);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1& 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

There is also a shorthand form to define a matrix column by column. 

> B := <<1, 4> |<3, 7>>;
\[ B := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

Or row by row.

> D := <<1| 4 > ,<3| 7>>;
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

There are more sophisticated methods to create matrices.

> DiagonalMatrix([A, B]);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 7 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

Here are some of the operations on matrices.

> C := MatrixInverse(B);
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{7}{5} & \frac{3}{5} \\ \frac{4}{5} & -\frac{1}{5} \end{bmatrix} \] 
> Transpose(B);
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> P, L, U := LUDecomposition(A); P.L.U ;
\[ \begin{split} & P, L, U := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \\ & \qquad \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 7 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

There are a lot more functions in the library LinearAlgebra. For instance, Adjoint, Basis, CharacteristicPolynomial, ColumnDimension, ColumnSpace, Determinant, Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, GaussianElimination, GramSchmidt, JordanForm, MatrixExponential, NullSpace, ReducedRowEchelonForm, and so on. We illustrate only two of these functions below.

Example: We consider the matrix \(A\) defined by the following Maple command.

> A:=Matrix([[4,15/7,-4/7],[-6,27/7,18/7],[16,-30/7,29/7]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix}4 & \displaystyle \frac{15}{7} & \displaystyle -\frac{4}{7} \\-6 & \displaystyle \frac{27}{7} & \displaystyle \frac{18}{7} \\ 16 & \displaystyle -\frac{30}{7} & \displaystyle \frac{29}{7} \end{bmatrix} \]

The eigenvalues of the matrix \(A\) are given by the following Maple function. 

>  eigva:=Eigenvalues(A);
\[ eigva := \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 3 - 6\, I \\ 3 + 6\, I \end{bmatrix} \]

The eigenvalues and some eigenvectors of the matrix \(A\) are given by the Maple function Eigenvectors().

>  eigvec:=Eigenvectors(A);
\[ eigvec := \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 3 + 6\, I \\ 3 - 6\, I \end{bmatrix} , \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \frac{1}{4} & \displaystyle -\frac{2}{13} + \frac{3\, I}{13} & \displaystyle -\frac{2}{13} - \frac{3\, I}{13} \\ \displaystyle \frac{1}{2} & \displaystyle -\frac{4}{13} - \frac{7\, I}{13} & \displaystyle -\frac{4}{13} + \frac{7\, I}{13} \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The function Eigenvectors()r returns a column vector listing all the eigenvalues (repeated \(k\) times if the algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue is \(k\)) and a matrix where the \(i^{th}\) column is an eigenvector (or a column of zeros if the geometric multiplicity is less than the algebraic multiplicity) associated to the \(i^{th}\) component of the column vector listing the eigenvalues. The set of eigenvectors is linearly independent. For instance, the first column of the matrix is an eigenvector associated to the eigenvalue in the first component of the column vector, the second column of the matrix (if not null) is an eigenvector associated to the eigenvalue in the second component of the column vector, and so on. In the previous example, \(\displaystyle \begin{pmatrix} 1/4 \\ 1/2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \) is an eigenvector of \(A\) associated to the eigenvalue \(6\), \(\begin{pmatrix} -2/13 + 3i/13 \\ -4/13 - 7i/13 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) associated to the eigenvalue \(3 + 6i\), and \(\begin{pmatrix} -2/13 - 3i/13 \\ -4/13 + 7i/13 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) associated to the eigenvalue \(3 - 6i\).

There is a more natural way to present the previous information, in particular when there are eigenvalues whose algebraic multiplicity is greater than their geometric multiplicity. With the option output=list, each element of the returned list is associated to a distinct eigenvalue. Each element of the list is formed of an eigenvalue, its algebraic multiplicity and a linearly independent set of eigenvectors associated to the eigenvalue.

Example: 

> eigvec := Eigenvectors(A,output=list);
\[ eigvec := \left[ \left[ 3 + 6\,I, 1, \left[ \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle -\frac{2}{13} + \frac{3\,I}{13} \\ \displaystyle -\frac{4}{13} - \frac{7\,I}{13} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right]\right], \left[ 3 - 6\,I, 1, \left[ \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle -\frac{2}{13} - \frac{3\,I}{13} \\ \displaystyle -\frac{4}{13} + \frac{7\,I}{13} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right]\right], \left[ 6 , 1 , \left[ \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \frac{1}{4} \\ \displaystyle \frac{1}{2} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right]\right] \right] \]

The exact values of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are often very complicated algebraic expressions. If it is sufficient, then we may only request real/complex approximations of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Example: Consider the following matrix \(A\).

> A:=Matrix([[2,5,4],[-4,2,1],[2,-1,1]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 & 4 \\-4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

If we use the commands Eigenvalues(A); or Eigenvectors(A);, then we get very ugly expressions even after simplifications with the function simplify(). It is then better to use the following commands. 

> evalf(Eigenvalues(A)); evalf(Eigenvectors(A));
\[ \begin{split} &\begin{bmatrix} 2.440084089 \\ 1.279957957 + 3.621504875\, I\\ 1.279957957 - 3.621504875\,I \end{bmatrix} \\ & \begin{bmatrix} 2.440084089 \\ 1.279957957 + 3.621504875\, I \\ 1.279957957 - 3.621504875\, I \end{bmatrix} , \\ & \begin{bmatrix} 0.3347749572 & -0.7298875032 + 1.442553403 \, I & - 0.7298875032 - 1.442553403 \, I \\ -0.7705341740 & -1.739732898 - 0.7363980506 \, I & -1.739732898 + 0.7363980506 \, I \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

It is even better to define \(A\) using real numbers by adding the decimal point. Then, all the computations are numerical computations and we automatically get the approximations of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Example:

\[ A := \begin{bmatrix}2.0 & 5 & 4 \\-4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Example: A model for the annual age distribution of a population is based on four age groups. For the nth year, let

  • \(x_1^{[n]}\) be the number of individuals of 20 years old and younger inclusively,
  • \(x_2^{[n]}\) be the number of individuals between 21 and 45 years old inclusively,
  • \(x_3^{[n]}\) be the number of individuals between 46 and 75 years old inclusively and
  • \(x_4^{[n]}\) be the number of individuals of 76 years old and older.

We assume that the evolution of the population is described by the linear iterative system

\[ \vec{x}^{[n+1]} = T \vec{x}^{[n]} \]

where

\[ T=\begin{pmatrix} 1-d_1-t_1+b_1 & b_2 & 0 & 0 \\ t_1 & 1-d_2-t_2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & t_2 & 1-d_3-t_3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & t_3 & 1-d_4\\ \end{pmatrix} \]

and

\[ \vec{x}^{[n]} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1^{[n]} \\ x_2^{[n]} \\ x_3^{[n]} \\ x_4^{[n]} \end{pmatrix} \ . \]

The parameters \(d_i\) and \(b_i\) are the mortality and birth rate respectively for the \(i^{th}\) age group; namely, on the \(n^{th}\) year, \(d_i x_i^{[n]}\) individuals from the \(i^{th}\) age group die and the members of this group give birth to \(b_i x_i^{[n]}\) individuals. Moreover, \(t_i\) is the transfer rate from the \(i^{th}\) age group to the \((i+1)^{th}\) age group; namely, on the \(n^{th}\) year, \(t_i x_i^{[n]}\) individuals from the \(i^{th}\) age group reach the age to join the \((i+1)^{th}\) age group. The matrix \(T\) is called the transfer matrix.

We assume that \(d_1=1/25, d_2=2/25, d_3=5/25\), \(d_4=8/25\), \(t_1=t_2=t_3=1/25\), \(b_0= 1/25\) and \(b_2=1/4\). We get the following transfer matrix. 

>  T := Matrix([[1-d1-t1+b1,b2,0,0],[t1,1-d2-t2,0,0],
      [0,t2,1-d3-t3,0],[0,0,t3,1-d4]]);
   Tb := subs({d1=1/25,d2=2/25,d3=5/25,d4=8/25,t1=1/25,t2=1/25,
      t3=1/25,b1=1/25,b2=1/4},T);
\[ \begin{split} & T := \begin{bmatrix} 1 - d1 - t1 + b1 & b2 & 0 & 0 \\ t1 & 1 - d2 - t2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & t2 & 1 - d3 - t3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & t3 & 1 - d4 \end{bmatrix} \\ & Tb := \begin{bmatrix} \frac{24}{25} & \frac{1}{4} & 0 & 0 \\ \frac{1}{25} & \frac{22}{25} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{25} & \frac{19}{25} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \frac{1}{25} & \frac{17}{25} \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

We suppose that the population distribution is initially given by 1000 individuals in the first age group, 1200 individuals in the second age group, 450 individuals in the third age group and 200 individuals in the fourth age group. We draw in the same figure a graph of the number of individuals in each age group for a period of 100 year.

>  x0 := Vector([1000, 1200, 450, 200]);
   Anu := proc(A, N, u)
      local i, V:
      V := u, u:
      for i from 2 to N + 1 do
         V := V,A.V[i]:
      od:
      RETURN(V[2 .. N + 2]):
   end proc:
   TbN := Anu(Tb,100,x0):
   plot1 := plot([seq([j,TbN[j][1]],j=1..100)], color=red):
   plot2 := plot([seq([j,TbN[j][2]],j=1..100)], color=blue):
   plot3 := plot([seq([j,TbN[j][3]],j=1..100)], color=green):
   plot4 := plot([seq([j,TbN[j][4]],j=1..100)], color=violet):
   display({plot1,plot2,plot3,plot4});
\[ x0 := \begin{bmatrix} 1000 \\ 1200 \\ 450 \\ 200 \end{bmatrix} \]

After a few years, we observe an exponential growth of the population. This is not a sustainable.

Note: We could have generated TbN with the command TbN:=seq(Tb^j.v0, j=0..100):. However, this is a costly way to generate TbN because it involves the computation of Tb^j for j=0..100. Thus, we are repeating multiple times the same matrix multiplications. Moreover, matrix multiplication is time consuming. We wrote a little procedure to reduce the number of matrix multiplications.

The line V := u, u: is the simplest way that we have found to initialize a list. The statement V := u: will not have initialized a list but a vector. The loop will then have not worked. We could have initialized V with the statement V:= u, A.u: if we assume that at least one iteration will always be requested. The rest of the procedure would then have to be replaced by for i from 2 to N do V := V,A.V[i]: od: RETURN(V[1 .. N + 1]):.

Example (continued): Is the model that we have used to describe the evolution of the population valid? If we suppose that the initial population distribution is given by \(x_1^{[0]} = 1000\) and \(x_2^{[0]} = x_3^{[0]} = x_4^{[0]} = 0\), then we should not expect any individual to be 45 years old or older before at least 25 years, should we?.

>  x00 := Vector([1000, 0, 0, 0]);
   evalf(Tb^(25).x00);
\[ \begin{split} & x00 := \begin{bmatrix} 1000 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \\ & \begin{bmatrix} 1359.504685 \\ 366.6737060 \\ 54.27563352 \\ 6.141708437 \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

Why does the model predict that after 25 years there will be some individuals older than 45 years old and even some older than 75 years old? The problem is that the model assumes uniform distribution of the population in each age group. For instance, the model does not differentiate between a 21 years old individual and a 22 years old individual, or even between a 21 years old individual and a 45 years old individual. Thus, when an individual from the first age group transfers to the second age group, this individual is partially counted as a 45 years old individual (a jump of 25 years). We observe the same phenomenon between the second age group and the third age group, and the third age group and the fourth age group. To be more realistic, our model should have age groups covering only one year.

We have also noted in the previous part of the example the problem related to the unsustainable exponential growth. This problem comes from the fact that we assume that the anuual growth rate of the population is linear. This produces an exponential growth of the population over time. This does not mean that the model should completely be rejected. Such models are still very good for some large insect populations (or populations of bacteria) and for a limited time period. Years have obviously to be replaced by a shorter unit of time.

Though it is not a problem in itself, we must also note that the data provided by the model include fractions of individuals. This obviously does not make sense. We have to treat the data as approximations of integer numbers of individuals. The model is most valuable for very large populations.

We now illustrate numerically that population growth is proportional to \(\lambda^n \vec{v} \) where \(\lambda\) is the largest eigenvalue of \(T\) in absolute value and \(\vec{v}\) is an eigenvector associated to \(\lambda\). The reader should prove this result rigorously if this has not already been done in one of their linear algebra courses. We first find the largest eigenvalue of \(T\) in absolute value and an eigenvector associated to it.

>  evT := Eigenvectors(Tb,output=list):
   findM := proc(evTlist)
      local i, eva, eve:
      eva := evTlist[1][1]:
      eve := evTlist[1][3][1]:
      for i from 2 to nops(evTlist) do
         if ( evalf(abs(evTlist[i][1])) > evalf(abs(eva)) ) then
            eva := evTlist[i][1]:
            eve := evTlist[i][3][1]:
         end if
      od:
      RETURN([eva,eve]):
   end:
   findR := findM(evT):
   eigvalM := findR[1];
   eigvctM := findR[2];
   eigvalMf := evalf(eigvalM);
   eigvctMf := evalf(eigvctM);
\[ \begin{split} & eigvalM := \frac{23}{25} + \frac{\sqrt{29}}{50} \\ & eigvctM := \begin{bmatrix} \frac{25\left(4 + \frac{\sqrt{29}}{2}\right) \left(6 + \frac{\sqrt{29}}{2}\right)} {4\left(-1 + \frac{\sqrt{29}}{2}\right)} \\ \left(4 + \frac{\sqrt{29}}{2}\right) \left(6 + \frac{\sqrt{29}}{2}\right) \\ 6 + \frac{\sqrt{29}}{2} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ & eigvalMf := 1.027703296 \\ & eigvctMf := \begin{bmatrix} 214.8190241 \\ 58.17582404 \\ 8.692582404 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

Note that, for small enough positive values of \(b_i, d_i\) and \(t_i\), all the eigenvalues of \(T\) are positive. So, we could have ignored the possibility of negative or complex eigenvalues in the procedure above (see note below). However, there is no harm done in writing a little more general procedure.

We compare \(T^{1000} \vec{x}^{[0]}\) to \( \lambda^{1000} \vec{v}\) and \(T^{2000} \vec{x}^{[0]}\) to \( \lambda^{2000} \vec{v}\).

>  TbNx0 := evalf(Tb^(1000).x0);
   seq(TbNx0[i]/eigvctMf[i],i = 1..4);
   seq(TbNx0[i]/(eigvalMf^1000 * eigvctMf[i]),i = 1..4);
   TbNx0 := evalf(Tb^(2000).x0);
   seq(TbNx0[i]/eigvctMf[i],i = 1..4);
   seq(TbNx0[i]/(eigvalMf^2000 * eigvctMf[i]),i = 1..4);
\[ \begin{split} & TbNx0 := \begin{bmatrix} 1.532783229 \times 10^{16} \\ 4.150979075 \times 10^{14} \\ 6.202357812 \times 10^{13} \\ 7.135230388 \times 10^{12} \end{bmatrix} \\ & 7.135230389 \times 10^{12}, 7.135230387 \times 10^{12}, 7.135230388 \times 10^{12}, 7.135230388 \times 10^{12} \\ & 9.675170398, 9.675170396, 9.675170396, 9.675170396 \\ & TbNx0 := \begin{bmatrix} 1.130394871 \times 10^{27} \\ 3.061258348 \times 10^{26} \\ 4.574106322 \times 10^{25} \\ 5.262079909 \times 10^{24} \end{bmatrix} \\ & 5.262079910 \times 10^{24}, 5.262079908 \times 10^{24}, 5.262079908 \times 10^{24}, 5.262079909 \times 10^{24} \\ & 9.675171744, 9.675171738, 9.675171740, 9.675171741 \end{split} \]

We could have taken any other large values for the exponent and we would have gotten similar results. We may therefore assume that \(T^n \vec{x}^{[0]} \approx 9.675171744\, \lambda^n \vec{v}\). Obviously, the constant of proportionality will be different if we choose another eigenvector \(\vec{v}\) associated to the eigenvalue \(\lambda\), or if we choose a different initial condition \(\vec{x}^{[0]}\).

Note: The reason why we wrote a procedure to find the largest eigenvalue in absolute value and an eigenvector associated to it is that Maple does not always list the eigenvalues and eigenvectors in the same order. So, the largest eigenvalue in absolute value can occupy a different position in the list depending on the problem (and even on the computer used). The procedure is not the most general procedure. It does not handle situations when there are more than one largest eigenvalue in absolute value or when there are more then one linearly independent eigenvector associated to the largest eigenvalue in absolute value. As an exercise, the reader is invited to generalize the procedure above.

Operations on the Components of a Matrix

It is possible to evaluate a function at each component of a matrix or vector. Consider the following function.

> mf:= x-> x^3-x;
\[ mf := x \mapsto x^3 - x \]

To evaluate the function mf at each component of B, we use the Maple function map( ).

> map(mf,B);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 24 \\ 60 & 336 \end{bmatrix} \]

Systems of Linear Equations

Consider the following system of linear equations.

> eq1 := 3*x+2*y-z=1; eq2 := -x+2*y+z=0; eq3 := -x-y-z=3;
\[ \begin{split} eq1 &:= 3 x+2 y-z = 1 \\ eq2 &:= -x+2 y+z = 0 \\ eq3 &:= -x-y-z = 3 \end{split} \]

To determine if the system has a unique solution, we can compute the determinant of the matrix formed by the coefficients.

> A : <<3,-1,-1>|<2,2,-1>|<-1,1,-1>>:
> Determinant(A)
\[ -10 \]

Since the determinant is different than zero, there is a unique solution. To find the solution, we use the function solve() of Maple.

> sol:=solve({eq1,eq2,eq3},{x,y,z});
\[ sol := \left\{z = -\frac{27}{10}, y = \frac{4}{5}, x = -\frac{11}{10} \right\} \]

To retrieve the values of x, y and z from this set, we use the function subs() of Maple.

> x := subs(sol,x); y := subs(sol,y); z := subs(sol,z);
\[ \begin{split} x &:= -\frac{11}{10} \\ y &:= \frac{4}{5} \\ z &:= -\frac{27}{10} \end{split} \]

Since the matrix A is invertible, we know from linear algebra that the solution of the system of linear equations is given by the following formula.

>  MatrixInverse(A).Vector([1,0,3]);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle -\frac{11}{10} \\ \displaystyle \frac{4}{5} \\ \displaystyle -\frac{27}{10} \end{bmatrix} \]

The function solve() of Maple can do a lot more than what we have shown above. For instance, we can solve the first two equations for \(x\) and \(z\) in terms of \(y\).

> solve({eq1,eq2},{x,z});
\[ \left\{x = -2\,y+\frac{1}{2}, z = -4\,y+\frac{1}{2}\right\} \]

Induced Norm of a Matrix

We can compute the induced norms of a square matrix. Consider the matrix \(C\) defined below.

> C:=Matrix([ [2,-1,0,0],[-1,2,-1,0],[0,-1,2,-1],
[0,0,-1,2]]);
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} 2& -1 & 0 & 0\\ -1 & 2& -1& 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 &-1\\ 0 & 0& -1& 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

The induced \(1\)-norm, \(2\)-norm and infinite norm of the matrix \(C\) are given by the following commands.

>  Norm(C,1);  Norm(C,2);  Norm(C,infinity);
\[ \begin{split} & 4 \\ &\sqrt{ RootOf(\_Z^{4}-23\,\_Z^3 + 130\_Z^2 - 176\_Z + 49, index =4)} \\ & 5 \end{split} \]

The floating point representation (an approximation) of the \(2\)-norm of \(C\) is 

> evalf(Norm(C,2));
\[ 3.897563191 \]

How are the induced norms computed? It can be shown that the \(1\)-norm of a \(n \times n\) matrix \(C\) is the maximum of \(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^n |c_{i,j}|\) for \(j=1, 2, \ldots, n\). Hence, the \(1\)-norm of the matrix \(C\) above is

> max( seq( add(abs(C[i,j]),i=1..4 ), j=1..4) );
\[ 4 \]

An equivalent formulation for the previous command is the following command.

> max(seq( Norm(SubMatrix(C,1..4,i),1),i=1..4));
\[ 4 \]

The function SubMatrix() is used to extract a sub-matrix from a given matrix. In the command above, SubMatrix(C,1..4,i) gives the \(i^{th}\) column of the matrix \(C\). The function Norm() with the option \(1\) compute the \(1\)-norm of each (vector) column obtained from the matrix \(C\); namely, compute the sum in absolute value of the components in each column of the matrix \(C\).

Similarly, it can be shown that the infinite norm of a \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) is the maximum of \(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^n |c_{i,j}|\) for \(i=1, 2, \ldots, n\). Hence, the infinite norm of \(C\) above is

> max( seq(add(abs(C[i,j]), j=1..4), i=1..4) );
\[ 4 \]

Finally, with a little bit more work than for the previous two norms, it can be shown that the \(2\)-norm of a \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) is the square root of the largest eigenvalue in absolute value of the matrix \(\displaystyle A A^\ast \). Hence, the \(2\)-norm of the matrix \(C\) above is

> tmp:=evalf(Eigenvalues(C.Transpose(C))):
> sqrt(max(seq(abs(tmp[i]),i=1..4)));
\[ 3.897563191 \]

Example (Conditioning Number): If \(A\vec{y} - \vec{b}\) is small, then does this mean that \(\vec{y}\) is closed to the solution of \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\)?

For instance, consider the matrix \(A\) defined by the command

> A:=Matrix([[3,6],[2.9999,6]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix} 3& 6\\ 2.9999 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \]

and the vector \(\vec{b}\) defined by the command

> b := Matrix([[9],[8.9999]]);
\[ b := \begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 8.9999 \end{bmatrix} \]

The vector \(\vec{y}\) defined by the command

> y:=Matrix([[3],[0]]);
\[ y := \begin{bmatrix} 3\\ 0\end{bmatrix} \]

seems to be a good approximation of the solution \(\vec{x}\) of \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\) because \(\| A \vec{y} - \vec{b}\|_\infty\) is small. In fact, Maple gives the following small value.

> Norm( A.y-b, infinity);
\[ 0.000200000000001310241 \]

However, this is not true because the real solution is obviously \(\displaystyle \vec{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\). The reason behind this behaviour is the large size of the conditioning number of the matrix \(A\). The conditioning number of the matrix \(A\), using the infinite norm, is defined as \(\| A^{-1} \|_\infty \|A \|_\infty\). The condition number is a way to compare the size in absolute value of the smaller eigenvalue with the largest eigenvalue of the matrix \(A\). The larger the condition number, the larger the difference in absolute value between these two eigenvalues. This has a very significant effect when solving systems of linear equations. A small change on the right hand side of the system of linear equations (i.e. \(\vec{b}\)) may have a very large impact on the solution. The condition number of the matrix \(A\) above is

> cond:=Norm(A,infinity)*Norm(MatrixInverse(A),infinity);
\[ cond := 180000.0000 \]

Since the condition number of \(A\) is large, we say that \(A\) is ill-conditioned.
