Linear Algebra

In this document, we briefly introduce the LinearAlgebra library that helps us perform standard operations in Linear Algebra.

Basic

The Maple functions for linear algebra are in the following library.

> with(LinearAlgebra):

Replace the colon at the end of the previous command by a semi-colon to get a complete list of the functions in that library.

It is easy to create (column) vectors and matrices.

> v := Vector([3, 2, -1]);
\[ v := \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> A := Matrix([[1 , 2 ,3],[2 ,3 ,4],[3,4,5]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \]

We can add and multiply vectors and matrices as long as we respects the rules about dimensions.

>  u := A v;
\[ u := \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 8 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> w := u + 2*v;
\[ w := \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 12 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix} \]

It is easy to retrieve and modify components of vectors or matrices.

> w[2]; A[2.3];
\[ \begin{split} & 12 \\ & 4 \end{split} \] 
> A[2,3] := 7; A;
\[ \begin{split} & A_{2,3} := 7 \\ & \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 7 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

Euclidean Geometry

The scalar product of the vectors u and v is computed with the following command.

>  DotProduct(u,v);
\[ 16 \]

The Euclidean norm, the 1-norm and the infinite norm of the vector v are:

> norm(v, 2); norm(v, 1); norm(v, infinity);
\[ \begin{split} \sqrt{14} \\ 6 \\ 3 \end{split} \]

The Cross product of u and v is computed with the following command.

> w := CrossProduct(u,v);
\[ w := \begin{bmatrix} -31 \\ 40 \\-16 \end{bmatrix} \]

It is well known that w is orthogonal to u and v. 

> DotProduct(w,u); DotProduct(w,v);
\[ \begin{split} & 0 \\ & 0 \end{split} \]

Matrix Operations

We have given above the basic method to define matrices in Maple. There are many more methods to define matrices. Special matrices may have their own functions in Maple to create them. Here are some examples.

> Id: = IdentityMatrix(3);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> Matrix(3, 4, {(1, 2) = 3, (1, 3) = 5, (3, 4) = 7}, fill = 1);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1& 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

There is also a shorthand form to define a matrix column by column. 

> B := <<1, 4> |<3, 7>>;
\[ B := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

Or row by row.

> D := <<1| 4 > ,<3| 7>>;
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

There are more sophisticated methods to create matrices.

> DiagonalMatrix([A, B]);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 7 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

Here are some of the operations on matrices.

> C := MatrixInverse(B);
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{7}{5} & \frac{3}{5} \\ \frac{4}{5} & -\frac{1}{5} \end{bmatrix} \] 
> Transpose(B);
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> P, L, U := LUDecomposition(A); P.L.U ;
\[ \begin{split} & P, L, U := \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \\ & \qquad \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 7 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

There are a lot more functions in the library LinearAlgebra: Adjoint, Basis, CharacteristicPolynomial, ColumnDimension, ColumnSpace, Determinant, Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, GaussianElimination, GramSchmidt, JordanForm, MatrixExponential, NullSpace, ReducedRowEchelonForm, and so on. We illustrate only two of these functions below.

We consider the matrix \(A\) defined by the command

> A:=Matrix([[4,15/7,-4/7],[-6,27/7,18/7],[16,-30/7,29/7]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix}4 & \displaystyle \frac{15}{7} & \displaystyle -\frac{4}{7} \\-6 & \displaystyle \frac{27}{7} & \displaystyle \frac{18}{7} \\ 16 & \displaystyle -\frac{30}{7} & \displaystyle \frac{29}{7} \end{bmatrix} \] }

The eigenvalues of the matrix \(A\) are given by the following Maple function. 

>  eigva:=Eigenvalues(A);
\[ eigva := \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 3 - 6\, I \\ 3 + 6\, I \end{bmatrix} \]

The eigenvalues and some eigenvectors of the matrix \(A\) are given by the Maple function Eigenvectors().

>  eigvec:=Eigenvectors(A);
\[ eigvec := \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 3 + 6\, I \\ 3 - 6\, I \end{bmatrix} , \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \frac{1}{4} & \displaystyle -\frac{2}{13} + \frac{3\, I}{13} & \displaystyle -\frac{2}{13} - \frac{3\, I}{13} \\ \displaystyle \frac{1}{2} & \displaystyle -\frac{4}{13} - \frac{7\, I}{13} & \displaystyle -\frac{4}{13} + \frac{7\, I}{13} \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

This function returns the list of eigenvalues (repeated \(k\) times if the algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue is \(k\)) and a matrix where each column is an eigenvector (or a column of zeros if the geometric multiplicity is less than the algebraic multiplicity) associated to the eigenvalue in the list occupying the same rank. The set of eigenvectors is linearly independent. For instance, the first column is an eigenvector associated to the eigenvalue in the first row of the first matrix, the second column is an eigenvector associated to the eigenvalue in the second row of the first matrix, and so on. In the previous example, \(\displaystyle \begin{pmatrix} 1/4 \\ 1/2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \) is an eigenvector of \(A\) associated to the eigenvalue \(6\), \(\begin{pmatrix} -2/13 + 3i/13 \\ -4/13 - 7i/13 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) associated to the eigenvalue \(3 -6i\), and \(\begin{pmatrix} -2/13 - 3i/13 \\ -4/13 + 7i/13 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) associated to the eigenvalue \(3 + 6i\).

There is a more natural way to present the previous information, in particular when there are eigenvalues whose algebraic multiplicity is greater than their geometric multiplicity. With the option output=list, each element of the returned list is associated to a distinct eigenvalue. Each element of the list is formed of an eigenvalue, its algebraic multiplicity and a linearly independent set of eigenvectors associated to the eigenvalue. 

> eigvec := Eigenvectors(A,output=list));
\[ eigvec := \left[ \left[ 3 + 6\,I, 1, \left[ \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle -\frac{2}{13} + \frac{3\,I}{13} \\ \displaystyle -\frac{4}{13} - \frac{7\,I}{13} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right]\right], \left[ 3 - 6\,I, 1, \left[ \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle -\frac{2}{13} - \frac{3\,I}{13} \\ \displaystyle -\frac{4}{13} + \frac{7\,I}{13} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right]\right], \left[ 6 , 1 , \left[ \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \frac{1}{4} \\ \displaystyle \frac{1}{2} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right]\right] \right] \]

The exact values of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are often very complicated algebraic expressions. If it is sufficient, then we may only request real/complex approximations of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors. For instance, consider the following matrix \(A\).

> A:=Matrix([[2,5,4],[-4,2,1],[2,-1,1]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 & 4 \\-4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

If we use the commands Eigenvalues(A); or Eigenvectors (A);, then we get very ugly expressions even after simplifications with the function simplify. It is then better to use the following commands. 

> evalf(Eigenvalues(A)); evalf(Eigenvectors(A));
\[ \begin{split} &\begin{bmatrix} 2.440084089 \\ 1.279957957 + 3.621504875\, I\\ 1.279957957 - 3.621504875\,I \end{bmatrix} \\ & \begin{bmatrix} 2.440084089 \\ 1.279957957 + 3.621504875\, I \\ 1.279957957 - 3.621504875\, I \end{bmatrix} , \\ & \begin{bmatrix} 0.3347749572 & -0.7298875032 + 1.442553403 \, I & - 0.7298875032 - 1.442553403 \, I \\ -0.7705341740 & -1.739732898 - 0.7363980506 \, I & -1.739732898 + 0.7363980506 \, I \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

It is even better to define \(A\) using real numbers by adding the decimal point. Then, all the computations are numerical computations and we automatically get the approximations of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors. 

> A:=Matrix([[2.0,5,4],[-4,2,1],[2,-1,1]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix}2.0 & 5 & 4 \\-4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] 
> Eigenvalues(A); Eigenvectors(A);
\[ \begin{split} &\begin{bmatrix} 1.2799579558670642 + 3.6215048733491346\,I\\ 1.2799579558670642 - 3.6215048733491346\,I \\ 2.4400840882658694 + 0\, I \end{bmatrix} \\ & \begin{bmatrix} 1.27995795586706 + 3.62150487334913\,I \\ 1.27995795586706 - 3.62150487334913\,I \\ 2.44008408826587 + 0\,I \end{bmatrix} , \\ &\begin{bmatrix} -0.0409862692988133 + 0.601839188895029\,I & -0.0409862692988133 - 0.601839188895029\,I & 0.256323997021917 + 0\,I \\ -0.704900923320764 + 0\,I & -0.704900923320764 + 0\,I & -0.589967663075411 + 0\,I \\ 0.343613224543820 - 0.145445373454335\,I & 0.343613224543820 + 0.145445373454335\,I & 0.765660606976777 + 0\,I \end{bmatrix} \end{split} \]

Operations on the Components of a Matrix

It is possible to evaluate a function at each component of a matrix or vector. Consider the following function.

> mf:= x-> x^3-x;
\[ mf := x \mapsto x^3 - x \]

To evaluate the function mf at each component of B, we use the Maple function map( ).

> map(mf,B);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 24 \\ 60 & 336 \end{bmatrix} \]

Systems of Linear Equations

Consider the following system of linear equations.

> eq1 := 3*x+2*y-z=1; eq2 := -x+2*y+z=0; eq3 := -x-y-z=3;
\[ \begin{split} eq1 &:= 3 x+2 y-z = 1 \\ eq2 &:= -x+2 y+z = 0 \\ eq3 &:= -x-y-z = 3 \end{split} \]

To determine if the system has a unique solution, we can compute the determinant of the matrix formed of the coefficients.

> A : <<3,-1,-1>|<2,2,-1>|<-1,1,-1>>:
> Determinant(A)
\[ -10 \]

Since the determinant is different than zero, there is a unique solution. To find the solution, we use the function solve() of Maple.

> sol:=solve({eq1,eq2,eq3},{x,y,z});
\[ sol := \left\{z = -\frac{27}{10}, y = \frac{4}{5}, x = -\frac{11}{10} \right\} \]

To retrieve the values of x, y and z from this set, we use the function subs() of Maple.

> x := subs(sol,x); y := subs(sol,y); z := subs(sol,z);
\[ \begin{split} x &:= -\frac{11}{10} \\ y &:= \frac{4}{5} \\ z &:= -\frac{27}{10} \end{split} \]

The function solve() of Maple can do a lot more than what we have shown above. For instance, we can solve the first two equations for \(x\) and \(z\) in terms of \(y\).

> solve({eq1,eq2},{x,z});
\[ \left\{x = -2\,y+\frac{1}{2}, z = -4\,y+\frac{1}{2}\right\} \]

Norm of a Matrix

We can compute the induced norms of a square matrix. Consider the matrix \(C\) defined below.

> C:=Matrix([ [2,-1,0,0],[-1,2,-1,0],[0,-1,2,-1],
[0,0,-1,2]]);
\[ C := \begin{bmatrix} 2& -1 & 0 & 0\\ -1 & 2& -1& 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 &-1\\ 0 & 0& -1& 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

The induced \(1\)-norm, \(2\)-norm and infinite norm of the matrix \(C\) are given by the following commands.

>  Norm(C,1);  Norm(C,2);  Norm(C,infinity);
\[ \begin{split} & 4 \\ &\sqrt{ RootOf(\_Z^{4}-23\,\_Z^3 + 130\_Z^2 - 176\_Z + 49, index =4)} \\ & 5 \end{split} \]

The floating point representation (an approximation) of the \(2\)-norm of \(C\) is 

> evalf(Norm(C,2));
\[ 3.897563191 \]

How are the induced norms computed? It can be shown that the \(1\)-norm of a \(n \times n\) matrix \(C\) is the maximum of \(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^n |c_{i,j}|\) for \(j=1, 2, \ldots, n\)$. Hence, the \(1\)-norm of the matrix \(C\) above is

> max( seq( add(abs(C[i,j]),i=1..4 ), j=1..4) );
\[ 4 \]

An equivalent formulation for the previous command is

> max(seq( Norm(SubMatrix(C,1..4,i),1),i=1..4));
\[ 4 \]

The function SubMatrix() is used to extract a sub-matrix from a given matrix. In the command above, SubMatrix(C,1..4,i) gives the \(i^{th}\) column of the matrix \(C\). The function Norm() with the option \(1\) compute the \(1\)-norm of each (vector) column obtained from the matrix \(C\); namely, compute the sum in absolute value of the components in each column of the matrix \(C\).

Similarly, it can be shown that the infinite norm of a \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) is the maximum of \(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^n |c_{i,j}|\) for \(i=1, 2, \ldots, n\). Hence, the infinite norm of \(C\) above is

> max( seq(add(abs(C[i,j]), j=1..4), i=1..4) );
\[ 4 \]

Finally, with a little bit more work than for the previous two norms, it can be shown that the \(2\)-norm of a \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) is the square root of the largest eigenvalue in absolute value of the matrix \(\displaystyle A^\ast A\). Hence, the \(2\)-norm of the matrix \(C\) above is

> tmp:=evalf(Eigenvalues(C.Transpose(C))):
> sqrt(max(seq(abs(tmp[i]),i=1..4)));
\[ 3.897563191 \]

Example (Conditioning Number): If \(A\vec{y} - \vec{b}\) is small, then does this mean that \(\vec{y}\) is closed to the solution of \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\)?

For instance, consider the matrix \(A\) defined by the command

> A:=Matrix([[3,6],[2.9999,6]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix} 3& 6\\ 2.9999 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \]

and the vector \(\vec{b}\) defined by the command

> b := Matrix([[9],[8.9999]]);
\[ b := \begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 8.9999 \end{bmatrix} \]

The vector \(\vec{y}\) defined by

> y:=Matrix([[3],[0]]);
\[ y := \begin{bmatrix} 3\\ 0\end{bmatrix} \]

seems to be a good approximation of the solution \(\vec{x}\) of \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\) because \(\| A \vec{y} - \vec{b}\|_\infty\) is small. In fact, Maple gives the following small value.

> Norm( A.y-b, infinity);
\[ 0.000200000000001310241 \]

However, this is not true because the real solution is obviously \(\displaystyle \vec{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\). The reason behind this behaviour is the large size of the conditioning number of the matrix \(A\). The conditioning number of the matrix \(A\), using the infinite norm, is defined as \(\| A^{-1} \|_\infty \|A \|_\infty\). The condition number is a way to compare the size in absolute value of the smaller eigenvalue with the largest eigenvalue of the matrix \(A\). The larger the condition number, the larger the difference in absolute value between these two eigenvalues. This has a very significant effect when solving systems of linear equations. A small change on the right hand side of the system of linear equations may have a very large impact on the solution. The condition number of the matrix \(A\) above is

> cond:=Norm(A,infinity)*Norm(MatrixInverse(A),infinity);
\[ cond := 180000.0000 \]

Since the condition number of \(A\) is large, we say that \(A\) is ill-conditioned.
