Systems of ODE

General Concepts

Consider the general system of ODE \(X'(t) = f(t,X(t))\) where \(f:\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n\). Our goal is to find \(X:\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n\) satisfying this system of ODE with an initial condition \(X(0) = X_0\). The theory says that there is always a solution if \(f\) is Lipshitz continuous and that this solution is unique. If \(f\) does not depend of the time \(t\), we say that the system of ODE is autonomous. If \(X\) is a solution of the system of ODE, then \(f(X(t))\) is the direction and velocity of \(X\) at time \(t\). In particular, \(f(X(t))\) is tangent to \(X\) at \(t\).

For autonomous systems in two ODE, it is traditional to draw the vector field associated to the system of ODE to get an idea of its global behaviour. Namely, for many (equally spaced) points \(X\) in the plane, we draw from each of them a vector in the direction \(f(X)\). Sometimes the length of the vector drawn is proportional to the norm of \(f(X)\).

A system of ODE of the form \(X'(t) = A X(t)\), where \(A\) is an \(n \times n)\) matrix is called a (homogeneous) linear system of ODE. The theoretical solution of this system is \(X(t) = e^{t A} X(0)\), where the exponential of a matrix \(B\) is defined by \(\displaystyle e^B = \sum_0^\infty \frac{1}{n!} B^n\). This series always converges. The notion of convergence for series of matrices is defined with any of the matrix norms. Consider the matrix

> with(LinearAlgebra): A := Matrix([[2,-1],[1,2]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

The exponential of this matrix is

>  expA := MatrixExponential(A);
\[ expA := \begin{bmatrix} e^2 \cos(1) & -e^2 \sin(1) \\ e^2 \sin(1) & e^2 \cos(1) \end{bmatrix} \]

and \(e^{tA}\) is given by 

>  exptA := MatrixExponential(A,t);
\[ exptA := \begin{bmatrix} e^{2t} \cos(t) & -e^{2t} \sin(t) \\ e^{2t} \sin(t) & e^{2t} \cos(t) \end{bmatrix} \]

We can use dsolve( ) to solve the system of homogeneous linear ODE \(X'(t) = A X(t)\).

> eq1 := {diff(x1(t),t) = 2*x1(t) - x2(t), diff(x2(t),t) = x1(t) + 2*x2(t)};
\[ eq1 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x1(1) = 2 x1(1) - x2(t) , \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x2(t) = x1(t) + 2 x2(t) \right\} \]

The general solution is

>  X := dsolve(eq1, {x1(t), x2(t)});
\[ X := \left\{ x1(t) = e^{2t} (\_C2 \cos(t) + \_C1 \sin(t), x2(t) = -e^{2t} ( \cos(t)\_C1 - \sin(t) \_C2 \right\} \]

where _C1 and _C2 are arbitrary constants. This is \(e^{tA} \begin{bmatrix} \_C2 \\ -\_C1 \end{bmatrix}\). We can also solve \(X'(t) = A X(t)\) with the initial condition \(X(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\).

>  eq2 := eq1 union {x1(0)=2,x2(0)=3};
\[ eq2 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x1(1) = 2 x1(1) - x2(t) , \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x2(t) = x1(t) + 2 x2(t) , x1(0) = 2, x2(0) = 3\right\} \]

The solution of this system is

>  X := dsolve(eq2,{x1(t),x2(t)});
\[ X := \left\{ x1(t) = e^{2t} (2 \cos(t) - 3 \sin(t) ), x2(t) = -e^{2t} ( -3 \cos(t) - 2 \sin(t) )\right\} \]

It is the case with _C1 = -3 and _C2 = 2 in the general solution.

We can draw the orbit of this solution.

> X1:=subs(X,x1(t)); X2:=subs(X,x2(t)); plot([X1,X2,t=-1..5],axes=FRAME,labels=['x1','x2']);
\[ \begin{split} & x1(t) = e^{2t} (2 \cos(t) - 3 \sin(t) ) \\ & x2(t) = -e^{2t} ( -3 \cos(t) - 2 \sin(t) ) \end{split} \]

Matrix Exponential Solutions

To evaluate the solution of an homogeneous linear system of ODE at various values of the independent variable, one has to evaluate \(e^{tA} X_0\) at several values of \(t\). In general, it is difficult and time consuming to compute the exponential \(e^{tA}\) when the dimension of the square matrix \(A\) is large. There are however some nice results from linear algebra that help us compute this exponential. For instance, it is possible to write \(A\) as \(A = U D V\), where \(D\) is a block diagonal matrix (e.g. a Jordan normal form or a real canonical form), and \(U\) and \(V\) are invertible matrices. We then have that \(e^{tA} = U e^{tD} V\), where \(e^{tD}\) is much easier and faster to compute than \(e^{tA}\) because \(D\) has zero components off the (block) diagonal (at lot of zero components). It is easy to multiply matrices with a lot of zeros at known locations.

We will not consider the general theory of matrix decomposition in this introduction. We will consider only \(2 \times 2\) matrices.

There could be:

  • one or two real eigenvalues with two linearly independent real eigenvectors associates to them,
  • one real eigenvalues with only one linearly independent real eigenvector associated to it, or
  • two complex (i.e. in \(\mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{R}\)) conjuagte eigenvectors with two linearly independent complex eigenvectors associates to them.

Two Linearly Independent Real Eigenvectors

Consider the system of ODE

> eq3 := {diff(x1(t),t)=-4*x1(t)+ 2*x2(t), diff(x2(t),t)=-1*x1(t)-x2(t)};
\[ eq3 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x1(t) = 4\; x1(t) + 2\; x2(t) , \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x2(t) = - x1(t) - x2(t) \right\} \]

The matrix of coefficients is

> A := Matrix([[-4,2],[-1,-1]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix}-4 & 2 \\ -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A are

> with(LinearAlgebra): Eigenvectors(A);
\[ \begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

We have two eigenvalues \(-3\) and \(-2\). An eigenvector associated to \(-3\) is given by the first column of the \(2\times 2\) matrix and an eigenvector associate to \(-2\) is given by the second column. This \(2\times 2\) matrix can be selected as the matrix \(U\) in the decomposition of \(A\) presented above. One can use dsolve( ) to solve this linear system of ODE. We are more interested in the qualitative aspect of the solutions. So, we plot the vector field associated to this system.

> with(DEtools): DEplot(eq3,[x1(t),x2(t)],t=0..5, [[x1(0)=0.5,x2(0)=0], [x1(0)=-0.5,x2(0)=-0.1]], linecolor=[blue,red], thickness=1,color=green, arrows=small,stepsize=0.01);

We have also drawn two orbits associated to the solutions with initial condition \(\displaystyle \begin{bmatrix}0.5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) (in blue) and \(\displaystyle \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) (in red). As can be seen from the vector field, all the solutions converge toward the origin. This is typical of linear system of ODE with two negative eigenvalues. The origin is called a sink or stable node. If the eigenvalues had both been positive, we would have observed that all solutions go away from the origin. The origin is then called a source or unstable node.

Note: Another way to draw the vector field is with the function dfieldplot( ).

> dfieldplot(eq3,[x1(t),x2(t)],t=-2..2,x1=-1..1,x2=-1..1):

We let the readers try it for themselves. We also let the readers the pleasure of discovering the qualitative aspect of the linear system of ODE when one of the eigenvalues is positive and one is negative. The origin is then called a saddle point.

Only One Linearly Independent Eigenvector

We must have an eigenvalue of algebraic multiplicity two and geometric multiplicity one. Consider the homogeneous system of linear ODE:

> eq4:={diff(x1(t),t)=3*x1(t)+x2(t), diff(x2(t),t)=3*x2(t)};
\[ eq4 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x1(t) = 3\; x1(t) + x2(t) , \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x2(t) = 3\; x2(t) \right\} \]

The matrix of coefficients is

> A := Matrix([[3,1],[0,3]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix}-4 & 2 \\ -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A are

> Eigenvectors(A);
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

As expected, there is an eigenvalue of algebraic multiplicity two and geometric multiplicity one. To get an idea of the qualitative behaviour of this linear system of ODE, we plot its vector field with the orbit of a couple of particular solutions.

> DEplot(eq4,[x1(t),x2(t)],t=-1..1,[[x1(0)=2,x2(0)=-3],[x1(0)=-2,x2(0)=2]],linecolor=[blue,green],thickness=1,stepsize=0.01);

As can be seen from the vector field, all the solutions go away from the origin. The origin is a unstable node. If the eigenvalue had been negative, all the solutions would have converged to the origin. The origin would then have been a stable node.

No Real Eigenvectors

Consider the homogeneous system of ODE

> eq5:={diff(x1(t),t)=-2*x1(t)-x2(t), diff(x2(t),t)=x1(t)-2*x2(t)};
\[ eq5 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x1(t) = -0.2\; x1(t) - x2(t) , \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x2(t) = x1(t) - 0.2\; x2(t) \right\} \]

The matrix of the coefficients of this system is

> A := Matrix([[-1/5,-1],[1,-1/5]]);
\[ A := \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{1}{5} & -1 \\ 1 & -\frac{1}{5} \end{bmatrix} \]

whose eigenvalues and eigenvectors are

>  with(LinearAlgebra): Eigenvectors(A);
\[ \begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{5} + I \\ -\frac{1}{5} - I \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} I & 1 \\ -I & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

There are no real eigenvalues and eigenvectors. It is taught in a basic ODE course that the general solution involves terms of the form \(e^{-0.2 t}\cos(t)\) and \(e^{-0.2 t}\sin(t)\).

>  dsolve(eq5, {x1(t), x2(t)});
\[ \left\{ x1(t) = e^{-\frac{t}{5}} (\_C2 \cos(t) + \_C1 \sin(t)), x2(t) = -e^{-\frac{t}{5}} (\cos(t)\_C1 - \sin(t) \_C2) \right\} \]

Again, to get an idea of the qualitative behaviour of this system of ODE, we plot its vector field. We also plot some particular solutions.

> DEplot(eq5,[x1(t),x2(t)],t=0..10,[[x1(0)=0.1,x2(0)=-0.1],[x1(0)=-0.1,x2(0)=0.1]],linecolor=[blue,green],thickness=1, stepsize=0.01);

As can be seen from the vector field, all the solutions converge toward the origin. The origin is called a stable spiral or stable focus. If the real part of the pair of complex eigenvalues had been positive, all the solutions would have gone away of the origin. The original would then be a unstable spiral or unstable focus.

Nonlinear Systems of ODE

There are no general methods to solve nonlinear systems of ODE explicitly. We often have no choice but to do a qualitative study of the system and/or solve the system numerically.

Consider the system of non-autonomous ODE

>  eq6 := {diff(x1(t),t)=-2*x1(t)-2*x2(t)+sin(2*t), diff(x2(t),t)=4*x1(t)+2*x2(t)+cos(2*t)};
\[ eq6 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x1(t) = -2\; x1(t) - 2\; x2(t) + \sin( 2t) , \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x2(t) = 4\; x1(t) + 2\; x2(t) + \cos(2 t) \right\} \]

We can use dsolve( ) to find the general solution of this system of ODE but more interestingly we can draw the orbit of some solutions to get a qualitative description of the behaviour of the system of ODE.

>  DEplot(eq6,[x1(t),x2(t)],t=0..5,[[x1(0)=1,x2(0)=-1],[x1(0)=-1,x2(0)=1],[x1(0)=2,x2(0)=2]],linecolor=[blue,green,red],thickness=1,stepsize=0.01,numpoints = 100, method = taylorseries);

It is interesting to note that none of the integration methods of Maple could properly draw orbits of solutions starting near the origin (at least, when these notes were written).

Note: One can find in basic ODE textbook a method to find the explicit solution of system of ODE like the one that we have just considered.

Higher Order ODE

It is possible to reduce an higher order ODE of the form

\[ y^{n}(t) + a_{n-1} y^{n-1}(t) + ... + a_1 y'(t) + a_0 y(t) = f(t) \]

to a system of first order ODE. It suffices to define \(X_1(t)= y(t), X_2(t)= y'(t), \ldots, X_n(t)= y^{n-1}(t)\) to get the system

\[ \begin{split} X_1'(t) &= X_2(t) \\ X_2'(t) &= X_3(t) \\ \ldots &\qquad \ldots \\ X_n'(t) &= y^{n}(t) = -a_{n-1} y^{n-1}(t) - ... - a_1 y'(t) - a_0 y(t) + f(t) \\ & = -a_{n-1} X_n(t) - ... - a_1 X_2(t) - a_0 X_1(t) + f(t) \end{split} \]

We have seen in the second order ODE

\[ \frac{\text{d}^2}{\text{d}t^2} x(t) + \cos(t) x(t) = 0 \]

This ODE can be reduced to the system of first-order ODE.

> eq1:= { diff(x1(t),t)=x2(t), diff(x2(t),t)=-cos(t)*x1(t)};
\[ eq1 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x1(t) = x2(t), \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d} t} x2 = -\cos(t) x1(t) \right\} \]

using the technique presented above. Again, we will not try to find the explicit solution using dsolve( ) but focus on the numerical solution given an initial condition.

>  SYST := eq1 union {x1(0)=0,x2(0)=1}:
> X := dsolve(SYST,{x1(t),x2(t)},type=numeric,output=listprocedure);
\[ X := [t = proc(t) ... end proc, x1(t) = proc(t) ... end proc, x2(t) = proc(t) ... end proc] \]

A plot of \(t \mapsto (x1(t),x2(t))\) is given by

> with(plots): odeplot(X,[x1(t),x2(t)],0..4*Pi,labels=[`x(t)`,`x'(t)`]);

This orbit has already been observed. It is the plot of \( t \mapsto (x(t), x'(t))\) for the intrudoctory example of second order ODE given in

.

Applications

Van der Pol Equation

Consider the system of ODE associated to the Van der Pol equation \(x''(t)+ \epsilon x'(t) (x^2(t)-1) + x(t) = 0\).

>  eq2 := { diff(x1(t),t) = x2(t), diff(x2(t),t) = -epsilon*x2(t)*(x1(t)^2-1)-x1(t)};
\[ eq2 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x1 = x2(t), \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x2(t) = -\epsilon x2(t) (x1(t)^2-1)-x1(t) \right\}; \]

This is a system of non-linear ODE. Assume that \(\epsilon=0.1\).

> eq3 := subs(epsilon=0.1, eq2);
\[ eq2 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x1 = x2(t), \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} x2(t) = -0.1 x2(t) (x1(t)^2-1)-x1(t) \right\}; \]

Since it is going to be instructive to plot the solution for many initial conditions, we write a procedure to produce these orbits.

> graph := proc (a, b, T, clr) local X; X := dsolve(eq3 union {x1(0) = a, x2(0) = b}, {x1(t), x2(t)}, type = numeric, output = procedurelist, method = classical[rk4], stepsize = 0.1e-1): RETURN(odeplot(X, [x1(t), x2(t)], 0 .. T, numpoints = floor(30*T), color = clr)): end proc;
\[ \begin{split} & graph := proc (a, b, T, clr)\\ & \qquad local\ X;\\ & \qquad X := dsolve(eq3\ union\ \{x1(0) = a, x2(0) = b\}, \{x1(t), x2(t)\}, type = numeric, output = procedurelist, \\ & \qquad \qquad method = classical[rk4], stepsize = 0.1e-1);\\ & \qquad RETURN(plots:-odeplot(X, [x1(t), x2(t)], 0 .. T, numpoints = floor(30*T), color = clr))\\ & end\ proc \end{split} \]

We now solve this system for many initial conditions (x1(0),x2(0)).

> gr1 := graph(4,1,5*Pi,"red"):
> gr2 := graph(-1,3,5*Pi, "blue"):
> display({gr1,gr2},axes=normal,labels=[`x1`,`x2`]);

> gr3:= graph(0.5,0.5,5*Pi,"red"):
> gr4:= graph(-1,1,5*Pi,"blue"):
> display({gr3,gr4},axes=normal,labels=[`x1`,`x2`]);

In the first figure, the orbits of the solutions seem to spiral toward the origin. However, in a the second figure they seem to spiral toward the infinity. This cannot go on forever because solutions cannot intersect and varies smoothly with respect to initial conditions. What is going on?

Let's look at the vector field associated to the system of ODE with some orbits of solutions.

> with(DEtools); DEplot(eq3, [x1(t), x2(t)], t = 0 .. 8*Pi, [[x1(0) = .5, x2(0) = .5], [x1(0) = 2, x2(0) = 2]], linecolor = [blue, red], thickness = 2, color = black, stepsize = 0.1e-1, arrows = small);

All the orbits of solutions converge toward a periodic solution. The outside orbit in the previous figure is getting very close to this periodic solution.

Note: There are several other Maple functions that may be used to plot the vector field: odeplot( ), and fieldplot if we consider that the right hand side of the system of ODE is a vector field.

Nonlinear Pendulum

Consider the pendulum made up of a very light rod of length \(L\) with a small ball of mass \(m\) at one end. 

> with(plots):with(plottools):
> gr1:=line([0,0], [0,-14], color=black, linestyle=3):
> gr2:=line([0,0], [3,-5], color=black):
> gr3:=disk([3,-5], 0.25, color=green):
> gr4:=arrow([3,-5], [1/2,-13/2], 0.02,0.3,0.2, color=black):
> gr5:=line([1/2,-13/2], [3,-32/3], color=black, linestyle=3):
> gr6:=arrow([3,-5], [3,-32/3], 0.02,0.3,0.1, color=black):
> gr7:=textplot([4,-9,"mg"], font=[COURIER,12],align={LEFT}):
> gr8:=textplot([-2,-4,"mg sin("], font=[COURIER,12],align={LEFT}):
> gr9:=textplot([-1.8,-4,'q'], font=[SYMBOL,12],align={LEFT}):
> gr10:=textplot([-1.6, -4, ")"], font = [COURIER, 12], align = {LEFT}):
> gr11:=arc([3,-35/3],2.5, Pi/2..3*Pi/5):
> gr12:=textplot([2.2,-8,'q'], font=[SYMBOL,12],align={RIGHT}):
> gr13:=arrow([-3,-4.5],[2,-5.4], 0.02,0.3,0.1, color=black):
> gr14:=textplot([2.1,-3,'L'], font=[COURIER,12],align={RIGHT}):
> display({gr1,gr2,gr3,gr4,gr5,gr6, gr7,gr8,gr9,gr10,gr11,gr12,gr13,gr14}, view=[-6..6,-14..0.5], axes=none, scaling=constrained);

Using Newton law force equals mass times acceleration, the angle θ is governed by the ODE 

> eq4 := m*L*diff(theta(t),t$2) = -k*L*diff(theta(t),t) -m*g*sin(theta(t));
\[ eq4 := m L \left( \frac{\text{d}^2}{\text{d}t^2} \theta(t) \right) = -k L \left(\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} \theta(t) \right) - m g \sin(\theta(t)) \]

where g = 9.8 m/s2 is the acceleration due to gravity and k is a constant of proportionality between the force due to friction and the velocity. We can write this ODE as a the system of ODE.

> eq5:={ diff(theta(t),t)=v(t), diff(v(t),t)=-(k*g/m)*v(t) -(g/L)*sin(theta(t))};
\[ eq5 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} \theta(t) = v(t), \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} v(t) = -\frac{k g v(t)}{m} - \frac{g \sin(\theta(t))}{L} \right\} \]

where v(t) is the angular velocity.

Ideal Pendulum

The ideal pendulum does not have friction, i.e. k=0. Let's suppose that L=3 m and m=0.5 kg (the mass does not matter if there is no friction.) 

> eq6 := subs({k=0, g=9.8, m=0.5, L=3}, eq5);
\[ eq6 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} \theta(t) = v(t), \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} v(t) = -3.266666667 \sin(\theta(t)) \right\} \]

We will plot the solution for the following two situations:

  • We let the pendulum loose from an angle of \(\pi/4\) so θ(0)=π/4 and v(0)=0.
  • We push the pendulum upward from an angle of \(\pi/4\) with a velocity of \(4\) m/s; so θ(0)= π/4 and v(0)=4 m/s.

Since we will plot the solution for different initial conditions, it is useful to re-introduce the procedure (after some small modifications) used before.

>  graph := proc (a, b, T, clr) local X: X := dsolve(eq6 union {theta(0) = a, v(0) = b}, {theta(t), v(t)}, type = numeric, output = procedurelist, method = classical[rk4], stepsize = 0.1e-1): RETURN(odeplot(X, [theta(t), v(t)], 0 .. T, color = clr)): end proc;
\[ \begin{split} & graph := proc (a, b, T, clr)\\ & \qquad local\ X;\\ & \qquad X := dsolve(eq6\ union\ \{theta(0) = a, v(0) = b\}, \{theta(t), v(t)\}, type = numeric, output = procedurelist,\\ & \qquad \qquad method = classical[rk4], stepsize = 0.1e-1);\\ & \qquad RETURN(plots:-odeplot(X, [theta(t), v(t)], 0 .. T, color = clr))\\ & end\ proc \end{split} \]

The orbits of the two solutions are draw in the following figure.

> pendul1 := graph(Pi/4,0,2*Pi,"red"):
> pendul2 := graph(Pi/4,4,Pi,"blue"):
> display({pendul1,pendul2}, axes=normal, labels=[`theta`,`v`]);

For the first case (in red), the pendulum oscillates back and forth for ever. For the second case (in blue), the pendulum go around for ever because our initial velocity was strong. Since there is no friction, the motion of the pendulum is for ever.

We plot the vector field with the orbits of the solutions.

> with(DEtools):
> DEplot(eq6,[theta(t),v(t)], t=0..1.5*Pi, [[theta(0)=Pi/4,v(0)=0], [theta(0)=Pi/4,v(0)=4]], linecolor=[blue,black], thickness=2, color=green, stepsize=0.01, arrows=small);

Pendulum with Friction

This time, we do not ignore the friction. We assume that k=0.1. We keep L=3 m and m=0.5 kg.

> eq6 := subs({m=0.5, g=9.8, L=3, k=0.1},eq5);
\[ eq6 := \left\{ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} \theta(t) = v(t), \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} v(t) = -1.960000000 v(t)-3.266666667 \sin(\theta(t)) \right\} \]

Because we have kept the name eq6 for this system, the procedure graph( ) defined above is still valid in the present context.

As for the ideal pendulum, we consider two sets of initial conditions.

  • We let the pendulum loose from an angle of \(\pi/4\) so θ(0)=π/4 and v(0)=0.
  • We push the pendulum upward from an angle of \(\pi/4\) with a velocity of \(8\) m/s; so θ(0)= π/4 and v(0)=8 m/s.

    The orbits of the two solutions are draw in the following figure.

    > pendul1:=graph(Pi/4,0,3*Pi,"red"):
> pendul2:=graph(Pi/4,8,3*Pi, "blue"):
> display({pendul1,pendul2}, axes=normal, labels=[`theta`,`v`]);

    We plot the vector field with the orbits of the solutions.

    > DEplot(eq6,[theta(t),v(t)], t=0..1.3*Pi, [[theta(0)=Pi/4,v(0)=0], [theta(0)=Pi/4,v(0)=8]], linecolor=[red,blue], thickness=2, color=black, stepsize=0.01, arrows=small);

    In the first case (in red), the oscillation of the pendulum is dying down. In the second case (in blue), after a complete rotation, the pendulum starts to oscillate and its oscillation is also dying down.
