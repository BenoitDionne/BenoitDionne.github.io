Complex Analysis

Other Topics

This document is a brief review of basic complex analysis. By default, Maple assumes that all variables are complex. So, we have been using complex expression since the beginning.

Complex Numbers

Any number of the form \(a+bi\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are real, is a complex number. The number \(i\) is the imaginary number such that \(i^2 = -1\). In Maple, the number \(i\) is denoted by the capital letter I.

> I; I^2;
\[ \begin{split} & I \\ & -1 \end{split} \]

We present some examples of the basic operations with complex numbers.

> z:=3+4*I; w:=-3-2*I; z+w; z-w; z*w; z/w;
\[ \begin{split} & z := 3 + 4\,I \\ & w := -3 -2\,I \\ & 2\,I \\ & 6 + 6\,I \\ & -1 -18\,I \\ & - {\displaystyle \frac {17}{13}} - {\displaystyle \frac {6}{13}} \,I \end{split} \]

Given a complex number \(z=a+b\,i\), the real number \(a\) is called the real part of \(z\) and the real number \(b\) is called the imaginary part. The Maple commands Re() and Im() are used to get the real and imaginary parts of a complex number. 

> Re(z); Im(z);
\[ \begin{split} & 3 \\ & 4 \end{split} \]

Any complex number \(z = a + b\,i\) defines a unique vector \(\vec{z} = (a,b)\) in the \(x,y\) plane (and vice-versa).

We can therefore identify any complex number to a unique vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Hence, any complex number \(z=a+b\, i \neq 0\) is completely determined by the length \(r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) of the vector \(\vec{z}\) from the origin to \((a,b)\) and the angle \(\theta\) between the vector \(\vec{z}\) and the \(x\) axis, measured counterclockwise from the \(x\) axis. The origin is an exception because \(r=0\) and \(\theta\) can be any angle. We have that \(a = r \cos(\theta)\) and \(b = r \sin(\theta)\). This gives the polar form

\[ z = r e^{\theta i} = r\cos(\theta) + i\, r\sin(\theta) \]

of the complex number \(z\). The polar form of a complex number is based on the formula \(e^{\theta i} = \cos(\theta) + i\,\sin(\theta)\) for all \(\theta\). The Maple function abs() gives the norm (length) of a complex number and argument() gives the value of the angle between the vector and the \(x\) axis, measured counterclockwise from the \(x\) axis. The value of the angle is between \(-\pi\) excluded and \(\pi\) included.

> r:=abs(z); theta:= argument(z);
\[ \begin{split} & r := 5 \\ & \theta := \arctan\left({\displaystyle \frac {4}{3}} \right) \end{split} \] 
> evalc(r*exp(theta*I));
\[ 3 + 4\,I \]

Complex Valued Functions

Consider the function defined in Maple by

> f:=w->w^2+3*w;
\[ f := w\rightarrow w^{2} + 3\,w \]

If we evaluate this function at \(z=3+4\,i\), then we get

> f(3+4*I);
\[ 2 + 36\,I \]

Suppose that \(z=x+y\,i\), where \(x\) and \(y\) are real. 

> assume(x,real); assume(y,real);

We then have that \(f(x+y i)\) is given by 

>  fr:=evalc(f(x+y*I));
\[ f := x{\scriptsize \sim}^{2} - y{\scriptsize \sim}^{2} + 3 \,x{\scriptsize \sim} + I\,(2\,x{\scriptsize \sim}\,y{\scriptsize \sim} + 3\,y{\scriptsize \sim}) \]

The symbol \(\scriptsize \sim\) after the variables \(x\) and \(y\) indicates that those are real variables. The real and imaginary parts of this function are 

>  Re(fr);Im(fr);
\[ \begin{split} & x{\scriptsize \sim}^2-y{\scriptsize \sim}^2+3 x{\scriptsize \sim} \\ & 2 x{\scriptsize \sim} y{\scriptsize \sim} +3 y{\scriptsize \sim} \end{split} \]

The derivative of a complex valued function \(f:\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}\) is defined by

\[ f'(z) = \lim_{w\to 0} \frac{f(z+w)-f(z)}{w} \]

if this limit exists. Note that \(w\) is in \(\mathbb{C}\) and can approach the origin from any direction. Suppose that \(f(z) = u(z) + i\, v(z)\) is differentiable, then

\[ \begin{split} u_x(z) &= v_y(z) \\ u_y(z) &= -v_x(z) \end{split} \]

These two equations are known as the Cauchy-Riemann equations. If \(u\) and \(v\) are differentiable real valued functions and the Cauchy-Riemann equations are satisfied, then the function \(f(z) = u(z)+i\,v(z)\) is a differentiable complex valued function. An amazing property of the complex valued functions is that if a function \(f:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow \mathbb{C}\) is differentiable, then it is infinitely differentiable.

Example: Consider the function 

> f:=z->z^2*(1-z);
\[ f := z\rightarrow z^{2}\,(1 - z) \]

We verify that \(f\) satisfies the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

> assume(x,real); assume(y,real);
> u:=Re(evalc(f(x+I*y))); v:=Im(evalc(f(x+I*y)));
\[ \begin{split} & u := (x{\scriptsize \sim}^2-y{\scriptsize \sim}^2) (-x{\scriptsize \sim}+1)+2 x{\scriptsize \sim} y{\scriptsize \sim}^2 \\ & v := 2 x{\scriptsize \sim} y{\scriptsize \sim} (-x{\scriptsize \sim}+1)-(x{\scriptsize \sim}^2-y{\scriptsize \sim}^2) y{\scriptsize \sim} \end{split} \] 
> diff(u,x); diff(v,y);
\[ \begin{split} & 2 x{\scriptsize \sim} (-x{\scriptsize \sim}+1)-x{\scriptsize \sim}^2+3 y{\scriptsize \sim}^2 \\ & 2 x{\scriptsize \sim} (-x{\scriptsize \sim}+1)-x{\scriptsize \sim}^2+3 y{\scriptsize \sim}^2 \end{split} \]

We do have that \(u_x(x,y) = v_y(x,y)\).

> diff(u,y); diff(v,x);
\[ \begin{split} & -2 y{\scriptsize \sim} (-x{\scriptsize \sim}+1)+4 x{\scriptsize \sim} y{\scriptsize \sim} \\ & 2 y{\scriptsize \sim} (-x{\scriptsize \sim}+1)-4 x{\scriptsize \sim} y{\scriptsize \sim} \end{split} \]

We also have that \(u_y(x,y)=-v_x(x,y)\).

Taylor and Laurent Series

Taylor Series

Suppose that \(f\) is a differentiable complex valued function defined on a disk \(D \subset \mathbb{C}\) of radius \(R\) centred at \(z_0\). The Taylor series of \(f\) at \(z_0\) is the series

\[ f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\,a_n (z-z_0)^n \qquad , \qquad z \in D \ , \]

where

\[ a_n = \frac{1}{n!}\,\frac{\partial^n}{\partial z^n}f(x_0) \ . \]

The series converges (pointwise) to \(f(z)\) for all \(z \in D\).

Remark: The formula for the Taylor series of an infinitely differentiable, real valued function \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) is almost identical to the formula above for the complex valued functions.

\[ f(x) \sim \sum_{n=0}^\infty\,a_n (x-x_0)^n \ , \]

where

\[ a_n = \frac{1}{n!}\,\frac{\partial^n}{\partial x^n}f(x_0) \ . \]

The big difference is the use of \(\sim\) for the Taylor series of the real valued function instead of the equality sign for the Taylor series of the complex valued function. The reason for this difference is that the Taylor series in the case of a real valued function may not converge for any \(x \neq x_0\) or, if it converges, it may not converge pointwise toward \(f\) in a neighbourhood of \(x_0\). For instance, if \(f:\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) is the function

\[ f(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2} & \quad \text{if} \quad x > 0 \\ 0 & \quad \text{if} \quad x \leq 0 \end{cases} \]

and \(x_0=0\), then the Taylor series of \(f\) near the origin is null because all derivatives of \(f\) at the origin are null but \(f\) is not null in the neighbourhood of the origin. This shows that the theory of Taylor series is simpler for complex valued functions than for real valued functions.

Consider the function \(f:\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}\) defined in Maple by 

> f:=z->sin(z^2-4);
\[ f := z\rightarrow \sin(z^{2} - 4) \]

The Taylor series of \(f\) at \(z=2\) is 

> unassign('z'):
> Tf:=series(f(z),z=2,10);
\[ \begin{split} & Tf := 4\,(z - 2) + (z - 2)^{2} - {\displaystyle \frac {32}{3}} \,(z - 2)^{3} - 8\,(z - 2)^{4} + {\displaystyle \frac {98}{15}} \,(z - 2)^{5} + {\displaystyle \frac {21}{2}} \,(z - 2)^{6} \\ & + {\displaystyle \frac {656}{315}} \,(z - 2)^{7} - {\displaystyle \frac {196}{45}} \,(z - 2)^{8} - {\displaystyle \frac {19151}{5670}} \,(z - 2)^{9} + \mathrm{O}((z - 2)^{10}) \end{split} \]

The terms in \((z-2)\) up to degree \(9\) (one less than the argument \(10\) in series() are explicitly computed. The term \( O((z-2)^{10})\) denotes the remainder of the Taylor series; the terms in \((z-2)\) of degree greater than or equal to \(10\). Because of the big \(O\) expression for the remainder of the Taylor series of \(f\), Tf cannot be used in computations. To use the Taylor series in computations, we have to truncate the series to the degree that we want. For instance, the truncation of order nine of the Taylor series of \(f\) is given by

> Tft:=convert(Tf,polynom);
\[ \begin{split} & Tft := 4\,z - 8 + (z - 2)^{2} - {\displaystyle \frac {32}{3}} \,(z - 2)^{3} - 8\,(z - 2)^{4} + {\displaystyle \frac {98}{15}} \,(z - 2)^{5} + {\displaystyle \frac {21}{2}} \,(z - 2)^{6} \\ & + {\displaystyle \frac {656}{315}} \,(z - 2)^{7} - {\displaystyle \frac {196}{45}} \,(z - 2)^{8} - {\displaystyle \frac {19151}{5670}} \,(z - 2)^{9} \end{split} \]

Hence 

> evalf(subs(z=2+I,Tft));
\[ - 23.85555556 + 15.73985891\,I \]

is an approximation of 

> evalf(f(2+I));
\[ - 22.97908558 + 14.74480519\,I \]

We may use the function taylor() instead of the function series() (with the same arguments) to get the Taylor series of a function. We can also compute any coefficient of the Taylor series using the function coeftayl(). For instance, the coefficient of \((z-2)^5\) in the Taylor series of \(f\) above at \(2\) is

> coeftayl(f(z),z=2,5);
\[ \frac{98}{15} \]

Laurent Series

Consider a function \(f:\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}\) and suppose that \(f\) has a singularity at \(z=z_0\) (i.e. \(f\) is not differentiable at \(z=z_0\)). It may still be possible to express \(f\) has a power series at the point \(z=z_0\). In fact, we may have

\[ f(z) = \sum_{-\infty}^\infty\,a_n (z-z_0)^n \]

for \(z\) in an annulus around the point \(z_0\) of the form \(0 < |z-z_0| < R\) for some \(R > 0\). We may have that \(a_n\) is non-zero for only a finite number of negative indices \(n\). If \(N\) is the smallest negative index such that \(a_N\) is non-zero, we say that \(z_0\) is a pole of order N. The series above is called the Laurent series of \(f\) at \(z_0\). The sum

\[ \sum_{-\infty}^{-1}\, a_n (z-z_0)^n \]

is called the principal part of the series. Consider the function \(f:\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}\) defined in Maple by 

> f:=z->sin(z)/(1-z^2);
\[ f := z\rightarrow \frac{\sin(z)}{1 - z^{2}} \]

Examples: The Laurent series of \(f\) at \(z=1\) is 

> Lf:=series(f(z),z=1,5);
\[ \begin{split} & Lf := - {\displaystyle \frac {1}{2}} \,\sin(1)\,(z - 1)^{-1} + \left( - {\displaystyle \frac {1}{2}} \,\cos(1) + {\displaystyle \frac {1}{4}} \,\sin(1)\right) + \left({\displaystyle \frac {1}{8}} \,\sin(1) + {\displaystyle \frac {1}{4}} \,\cos(1)\right)\,(z - 1) + \\ & \left( - {\displaystyle \frac {1}{24}} \,\cos(1) - {\displaystyle \frac {1}{16}} \,\sin(1)\right)\,(z - 1)^{2} + \left({\displaystyle \frac {1}{96}} \,\sin(1) + {\displaystyle \frac {1}{48}} \,\cos(1)\right)\,(z - 1)^{3} + \mathrm{O}((z - 1)^{4}) \end{split} \]

The principal part of this Laurent series is \(\displaystyle \frac{-\sin(1)}{2(z-1)}\). Hence, \(1\) is a pole of order one of \(f\). Consider the function \(f:\mathbb{C}\to \mathbb{C}\) defined in Maple by 

> f:=z->1/sin(z^3);
\[ f := z\rightarrow {\displaystyle \frac {1}{\sin(z^{3})}} \]

Its Laurent series at \(z=0\) is 

> series(f(z),z=0,10);
\[ z^{-3} + {\displaystyle \frac {1}{6}} \,z^{3} + \mathrm{O}(z^{4}) \]

Therefore, \(z=0\) is a pole of order three of \(f\).

The coefficient of the term in \( 1/(z-z_0)\) in the Laurent series of a function \(f\) at a point \(z_0\) is called the residue of \(f\) at \(z_0\). This coefficient is denoted \(\mathrm{res}(f,z_0)\). We have the following result

\[ \oint_C f(z)\ \text{d}z = 2\pi i \big( \mathrm{res}(f,z_1) + \mathrm{res}(f,z_2) + \ldots + \mathrm{res}(f,z_k) \big) \ , \]

where \(C\) is a closed curve in the complex plane, \(C\) is positively oriented and \(z_1, z_2, \ldots , z_k\) are all the poles of \(f\) inside the region bounded by the curve \(C\). If \(D\) is the region bounded by \(C\), the positive orientation is such that the region \(D\) is to the left of the curve \(C\) as we move along this curve.

The residues of \(f\) at \(z_0\) can be computed with the function residue() of Maple. Consider the function \(f:\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}\) defined in Maple by

> f:=z->1/sin(z);
\[ f := z\rightarrow {\displaystyle \frac {1}{\sin(z)}} \]

This function has poles at \(n\pi\) for \(n\) an integer. The residue of \(f\) at \(2\pi\) is 

> readlib(residue): residue(f(z),z=2*Pi);
\[ 1 \]

We can check this result by computing the Laurent series of \(f\) at \(2\pi\).

> series(f(z),z=2*Pi,5);
\[ (z - 2\,\pi )^{-1} + {\displaystyle \frac {1}{6}} \,(z - 2\,\pi ) + \mathrm{O}((z - 2\,\pi )^{3}) \]

The coefficient of \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{z-2\pi}\) is effectively \(1\).

Example Evaluate the integral of the function \(\displaystyle f(z) = \frac{1}{(3z-i)^2 \, \sin(z)}\) counterclockwise along the circle \(C\) of radius \(1\) centred at the origin; namely, evaluate

\[ \oint_C \frac{1}{(3z-i)^2 \, \sin(z)}\ \text{d}z \]

with the positive orientation on the curve defined by \(C = \{ z : |z| = 1 \}\).

In Maple, the function \(f\) is defined by

> f:=z->1/(sin(z)*(3*z-I)^2);
\[ f := z\rightarrow {\displaystyle \frac {1}{\sin(z)\,(3\,z - I)^{2}}} \]

The poles of \(f\) inside the unit disk (i.e. the region bounded by \(C\)) are \(0\) and \(i/3\). Since the orientation of \(C\) is positive, the value of the integral is

> evalf( 2*Pi*I*( residue(f(z),z=0) + residue(f(z),z=I/3)));
\[ .1119173282\,I \]

We can directly compute the integral. Since the orientation on \(C\) is counterclockwise, a parametric representation of the curve \(C\) is given by \(z = e^{t\, i}\) for \(0 \leq t < 2\pi\).

> r:=t->exp(t*I);
\[ r := t\rightarrow e^{(I\,t)} \]

for \(0 \leq t < 2\pi\). The value of the integral is 

> evalf( int((f@r)(t)*diff(r(t),t),t=0..2*Pi) );
\[ -2.962170701*10^{-14} + 0.1119173235\,I \]

For a numerical integration, this is close enough to the exact solution.

Example Evaluate the integral

\[ \int_{-\infty}^\infty\,\frac{\sin(x)}{(x-2)^2+4}\ \text{d}x \ . \]

We first note that this integral is the imaginary part of the integral

\[ \int_{-\infty}^\infty\, \frac{e^{ix}}{(x-2)^2+4}\ \text{d}x \]

which is the integral

\[ \int_C \,\frac{e^{iz}}{(z-2)^2+4}\ \text{d}z \ , \]

where \(C\) is the curve defined by \(z=x\) for \(-\infty < x < \infty\) and the direction along \(C\) is given by \(x\) increasing. The function \(\displaystyle \frac{e^{iz}}{(z-2)^2+4}\) has poles at \(z = 2+2\,i\) and \(2-2\,i\). Moreover

\[ \lim_{r\rightarrow \infty} \oint_{C_r}\,\frac{e^{iz}}{(z-2)^2+4}\ \text{d}z = \int_C \,\frac{e^{iz}}{(z-2)^2+4}\ \text{d}z \ , \]

where

\[ C_r = \{ x : -r \leq x \leq r\}\cup \{ re^{\theta i} : 0 < \theta < \pi \} \]

is the closed curve drawn below

because

\[ \lim_{r\rightarrow \infty} \int_{C_r'} \frac{e^{iz}}{(z-2)^2+4} \ \text{d}z = 0 \ , \]

where \(C_r'\) is the half circle that is a part of the curve \(C_r\). The proof that this limit is null uses techniques taught in a first course on complex analysis and relied on the fact that the integrand converges uniformly to \(0\) as \(r \to 0\).

\(C_r\) is a positively oriented closed curve surrounding the pole at \(2+2\,i\) if \(r\) is large enough. Hence, the value of the integral of 

> f:=z->exp(z*I)/((z-2)^2+4);
\[ f := z\rightarrow {\displaystyle \frac {e^{(I\,z)}}{(z - 2)^{2} + 4}} \]

along \(C_r\) is

> evalf(2*Pi*I*(residue(f(z),z=2+2*I)) );
\[ - 0.08846622805+ 0.1933022349\,I \]

if \(r\) is large enough and this integral is constant after that. Thus

\[ \int_C \,\frac{e^{iz}}{(z-2)^2+4}\ \text{d}z = \lim_{r\rightarrow \infty} \int_{C_r}\,\frac{e^{iz}}{(z-2)^2+4}\ \text{d}z = - 0.08846622805+ 0.1933022349\,i \]

Finally, we find that the value of the integral is

> Im(%);
\[ \displaystyle 0.1933022349 \]

We can verify our result with Maple because it can compute this improper integral.

> int(sin(x)/((x-2)^2+4),x=-infinity..infinity); evalf(%);
\[ \begin{split} & {\displaystyle \frac{\pi \sin(2) \mathrm{cosh}(2)}{2}} - {\displaystyle \frac{\pi \sin(2) \mathrm{sinh}(2)}{2}} \\ & 0.193302235 \end{split} \]

Conformal Mapping

A conformal Mapping is a function \(f:\mathbb{C}\to \mathbb{C}\) that preserves the angles between intersecting curves. If the derivative of \(f:\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}\) is never zero, \(f\) is a conformal mapping. An important class of conformal mappings are of the form

\[ z \mapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d} = \frac{a}{c} + \frac{bc-ad}{c} \frac{1}{cz+d} \ . \]

These transformations are called linear fractional transformations. A linear fractional transformation is a composition of a dilatation, \(cz\), followed by a translation, \(cz+d\), followed by an inversion, \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{cz+d}\), followed by a dilatation, \(\displaystyle \frac{bc-ad}{c} \frac{1}{cz+d}\), and followed by another translation, \(\displaystyle \frac{a}{c} + \frac{bc-ad}{c} \frac{1}{cz+d}\). Hence, linear fractional transformations preserve circles (a line is a circle of infinite radius.)

A linear fractional transformation is completely and uniquely determined by the image of three points. Consider the linear fractional transformation

\[ f(z) = \frac{2z - 3}{2z + 3} \ . \]

This function is defined in Maple by

> f:=z->(2*z-3)/(2*z+3);
\[ f := z\rightarrow {\displaystyle \frac {2\,z - 3}{2\,z + 3}} \]

To visualize the conformal aspect of this mapping, we plot the image of a rectangle by this mapping. The Maple function conformal() plots the image of rectangles. The rectangles are defined by two diagonally opposite corners. For instance, the image by \(f\) of the rectangle with diagonally opposite corners at \(0\) and \(2+2\,i\) is

> with(plots): conformal(f(z),z=0..2+2*I,scaling=constrained);

The blue and red lines are the image of horizontal and vertical lines respectively (can the reader say why it is so?).

To find the linear fractional transformation \(w=f(z)\) such that \(f(z_1)=w_1, f(z_2)=w_2\) and \(f(z_3)=w_3\), we solve the system of equations

\[ \frac{(w-w_1)(w_2-w_3)}{(w-w_3)(w_2-w_1)} = \frac{(z-z_1)(z_2-z_3)}{(z-z_3)(z_2-z_1)} \] for w.

Example: Find the linear fractional transformation \(f\) such that \(f(1)=i, f(2)=3\) and \(f(i)=2\). We have to solve

>  unassign('w'): unassign('z'):
> eq:=((w-I)*(3-2))/((w-2)*(3-I)) =((z-1)*(2-I))/((z-I)*(2-1)); f:=eval(solve(eq,z));
\[ \begin{split} & eq := {\displaystyle \frac {({\displaystyle \frac {3}{10}} + {\displaystyle \frac {1}{10}} \,I)\,(w - I)}{w - 2}} = {\displaystyle \frac {(2 - I)\,(z - 1)}{z - I}} \\ & f = \frac{43-19 \,I-41 z+23 \,I z}{21-13 \,I+11 \,I z-17 z} \end{split} \]

We verify that we have the right mapping.

> subs(z=1,f);subs(z=2,f);subs(z=I,f);
\[ \begin{split} I \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{split} \]

If we plot the image of the rectangle with opposite corners at \(0\) and \(1+1\,i\), then we get

> conformal(f,z=0..1+I,scaling=constrained);

Steady State Temperature

The steady state temperature (i.e. independent of time) in a thin plate is governed by the equation

\[ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}T(x,y) + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2}T(x,y) = 0 \ . \]

Functions \(T\) satisfying this equation are called harmonic functions. The reader should prove that the real and imaginary part of a differentiable complex valued function are harmonic using the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

Our goal is to find the steady state heat distribution in a thin plate defined for \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(y \geq 0\), and satisfying the boundary condition \[ T(x,y) = \begin{cases} 2 & \quad \text{if} \ |x|<1 \ \text{and} \ y=0 \\ 0 & \quad \text{if} \ |x|\geq 1 \ \text{and} \ y =0 \end{cases} \ . \]

We also assume that \(T(x,y)\) is bounded for \(y \geq 0\). Equivalently, we require that the temperature in the plate approaches zero as \(\|(x,y)\| \to \infty\).

The boundary condition can be interpreted as follows: the boundary is kept at a temperature of \(0\) degree except for the section between \(-1\) and \(1\) where it is kept at a temperature of \(2\) degrees.

This is the type of problems that we generally solve using techniques of PDE. However, this simple problem can also be solved using conformal mappings as we now show. We use a conformal mapping \(f\) to transform the domain \(z=x+i\,y\) with \(y \geq 0\) into the horizontal strip

\[ S = \{ w = u+i v : u \in \mathbb{R} \ \text{and} \ 0\leq v \leq \pi \} \ . \]

We solve the steady state equation on \(S\). In fact, we will see that it is easy to solve it on \(S\). Finally, we use the mapping \(f\) to transform this solution on \(S\) to a solution of the initial problem on the domain \(y\geq 0\).

We transform the upper complex plane \(z=x+i\,y\) with \(y\geq 0\) into the horizontal strip \(S\) with the transformation

\[ w = f(z) = \ln\left(\frac{z-1}{z+1}\right) \ . \]

This is a conformal mapping except at \(z=-1,1\). To understand why \(f\) is well defined for \(z \neq -1,1\) and maps the upper half-plane \(y \geq 0\) onto the horizontal strip \(S\), let \(\displaystyle z-1=r_1 e^{i\,\theta_1}\) and \(z+1=r_2 e^{i\,\theta_2}\).

We then have that

\[ w= f(z) = \ln\left(\frac{z-1}{z+1}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)+i(\theta_1-\theta_2) \]

with \(0 < \theta_1-\theta_2 < \pi\) and \(\ln()\) is well defined because \(r_1,r_2 > 0\) for \(z \neq -1,1\).

If \(z = x \in \mathbb{R}\) (i.e. \(z\) is on the \(x\) axis), we find that the line \(z=x\) with \(|x|\leq 1\) is mapped onto the entire line \(w=u+i\,v\) with \(v=\pi\), the line \(z=x\) with \(x<-1\) is mapped onto the line \(w=u\) with \(u>0\) and the line \(z=x\) with \(x>1\) is mapped onto the line \(w=u\) with \(u<0\). We may extend the transformation \(f\) to the compactify complex plane by saying that \(-1\) and \(1\) are mapped to \(\infty\), and \(\infty\) is mapped to \(0\).

We now prove that the solution of the heat equation in the strip \(S\) that satisfies the boundary condition is \(T(u,v)=2v/\pi\). The boundary condition for the domain \(y \geq 0\) are transformed by \(f\) into the conditions \(T(u,0) = 0\) and \(T(u,\pi) = 2\) for \(S\). The function \(T\) satisfies the steady state heat equation (i.e. \(T\) is harmonic inside \(S\)) because it is the imaginary part of the differentiable complex valued function \(g(w) = 2w/\pi\) where \(w=u+i\,v\). The boundary conditions are obviously satisfied.

Therefore, the solution \(T(x,y)\) for the original problem in the half plane \(y \geq 0\) is the imaginary part of the function \((g \circ f)(z)\).

> assume(x,real);assume(y,real);
> tmp:= (z-1)/(z+1);
\[ tmp := {\displaystyle \frac {z - 1}{z + 1}} \] 
> tmp:=simplify(evalc( subs(z=x+I*y,tmp) ));
\[ tmp := \frac{x{\scriptsize \sim}^{2} - 1 + y{\scriptsize \sim}^{2} + 2\,I\,y{\scriptsize \sim}}{x{\scriptsize \sim}^{2} + 2\,x{\scriptsize \sim} + 1 + y{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}} \] 
> w:=simplify(evalc( log(tmp) ));
\[ w := -\frac{\ln\left(x{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}+y{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}+2 x{\scriptsize \sim} +1\right)}{2}+\frac{\ln\left(x{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}+y^{2}-2 x{\scriptsize \sim} +1\right)}{2}+I \arctan \! \left(2 y{\scriptsize \sim} ,x{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}+y{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}-1\right) \] 
>  f:=2*w/Pi;
\[ f := \frac{2 \left(-\frac{\ln\left(x{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}+y{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}+2 x{\scriptsize \sim} +1\right)}{2}+\frac{\ln \left(x{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}+y{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}-2 x{\scriptsize \sim} +1\right)}{2}+ I \arctan\left(2 y{\scriptsize \sim} ,x{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}+y{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}-1\right)\right)}{\pi} \] 
> T:=evalc( Im(f) );
\[ T := \frac{2 \arctan\left(2 y{\scriptsize \sim} ,x{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}+y{\scriptsize \sim}^{2}-1\right)}{\pi} \]
